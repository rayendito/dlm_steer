Example 1

The human brain works through billions of neurons that communicate using electrical impulses and chemical neurotransmitters across synapses. Different regions handle different functions: the cerebral cortex supports thinking, memory, language, and decision-making; the cerebellum coordinates movement and balance; and the brainstem controls automatic processes like breathing and heart rate. Signals travel through neural networks, where synaptic plasticity allows the brain to learn, adapt, and store information over time.

The workers send messages to each other, so the city can learn and remember new things.

Photosynthesis is how plants, algae, and some tiny bugs turn sunlight into food. A special thing called chlorophyll catches the sunlight and uses it to split water and make special molecules. Then, they use these special molecules to turn carbon dioxide from the air into food that helps them grow and live.

A long time ago, many people were taken from Africa to the Americas. They worked on farms and made lots of money for the people who owned them. Some of them tried to escape and made new cultures in the Americas.

Example 75

Before

The Scientific Revolution promoted mathematical description, systematic observation, and experiments in studying nature. Thinkers such as Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton challenged inherited models, although their work grew from earlier European, Islamic, and classical traditions rather than appearing in isolation.

After

The Scientific Revolution helped people learn about nature by using math and doing experiments. Important people like Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton changed old ways of thinking, even though their ideas were based on old ideas from Europe, Islam, and Greece.

Example 76

Before

The Industrial Revolution shifted production toward mechanized factories powered first by water and coal. It increased output and urbanization, transformed transport and labor, and created new consumer goods, while workers faced dangerous conditions, pollution, crowded housing, and unstable employment.

After

The Industrial Revolution changed how things were made. Factories were built using water and coal. More things were made, and people moved to cities. But workers were not safe and dirty, lived in small houses, and had no jobs.

Example 77

Before

The French Revolution began amid fiscal crisis, social inequality, and demands for political representation. It abolished feudal privileges and proclaimed universal rights, but also passed through war, political terror, and authoritarian rule, leaving contested legacies of citizenship and nationalism.

After

The French Revolution started because people were unhappy with their money and wanted more say in government. It took away old rules and said everyone has rights, but it also had wars and scary times, leaving divided ideas about citizenship and being patriotic.

Example 78

Before

Nineteenth-century nationalism argued that people sharing language, culture, or history should form political communities. It helped unify states such as Italy and Germany and inspired anti-imperial movements, but it could also define minorities as outsiders and intensify competition among states.

After

In the 19th century, nationalism said people with similar language, culture, or history should work together. It helped countries like Italy and Germany become one and inspired freedom movements. But it could also create outsiders and make countries more competitive.

Example 79

Before

World War I grew from alliance commitments, militarism, imperial rivalry, nationalism, and the crisis following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand. Industrial weapons and trench warfare produced mass casualties, while the war toppled empires and redrew borders without resolving many underlying conflicts.

After

World War I started because of contracts between countries, big armies, and a problem when a prince was hurt. They used big guns and fought in trenches, and many people got hurt. Empires fell apart and borders changed, but it didn't solve many big problems.

Example 80

Before

Decolonization accelerated after World War II as independence movements challenged weakened European empires. New states emerged through negotiation, protest, and armed struggle, but colonial borders, economic dependency, internal divisions, and Cold War intervention complicated the transition to self-rule.

After

After a big war, many countries wanted to be free from European rulers. They made new countries by talking, protesting, and sometimes fighting. But it was hard because of old borders, money problems, big fights, and other countries helping.

Example 81

Before

Scarcity means that available resources cannot satisfy every possible use, so individuals and societies must make choices. The opportunity cost of a decision is the value of the best alternative given up, including costs that may not appear as direct monetary payments.

After

Scarcity means we don't have enough things to do everything we want, so we have to make choices. The opportunity cost is the best thing we miss when making a choice, even if it's not paid in money.

Example 82

Before

Demand usually falls as price rises because consumers substitute away or can afford less, while supply usually rises as higher prices make additional production worthwhile. Their intersection determines a market equilibrium, and shortages or surpluses create pressure for price and quantity to adjust.

After

When something costs more, people usually buy less of it, but companies might make more because it's worth more. This balance is what keeps the market fair. If there's too much or too little, the price and amount change.

Example 83

Before

Elasticity measures how strongly one economic variable responds to another. Price elasticity of demand compares the percentage change in quantity demanded with the percentage change in price, helping firms and policymakers anticipate effects on revenue, consumption, and tax burdens.

After

Elasticity shows how much one thing changes when another thing changes. Price elasticity of demand shows how much people buy when the price changes. It helps businesses and leaders know what happens to money, spending, and taxes.

Example 84

Before

Comparative advantage explains why specialization and trade can benefit participants even when one producer is more efficient at everything. Each side focuses relatively more on goods with lower opportunity cost, allowing combined output and potential consumption to increase.

After

Comparative advantage is when people focus on making things they do best and trade them with others. It helps everyone make more and have more to eat, even if one person is the best at everything.

Example 85

Before

Gross domestic product measures the market value of final goods and services produced within a country over a period. Real GDP adjusts for price changes and is useful for tracking output, but it does not fully capture household production, environmental damage, inequality, leisure, or well-being.

After

Gross domestic product is a way to measure how much money a country makes from selling goods and services. Real GDP helps us see how much money is made, but it doesn't count other important things like the environment, fairness, or what makes people happy.

Example 86

Before

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level that reduces money's purchasing power. It can result from strong aggregate demand, rising production costs, or persistent expectations, and unexpected inflation redistributes wealth between borrowers, lenders, workers, and firms.

After

Inflation is when prices go up and your money buys less. It can happen because too many people want things, making things more expensive, or people thinking it will. It can move money between different people, like you and your friends.

Example 87

Before

Unemployment statistics classify people as unemployed when they lack work, are available, and are actively seeking a job. Frictional unemployment reflects normal job search, structural unemployment reflects mismatched skills or locations, and cyclical unemployment rises when overall demand weakens.

After

Unemployment means people don't have a job but are trying to find one. There are different types of unemployment, like when people are looking for jobs, when their skills don't match the jobs, or when the economy has fewer jobs.

Example 88

Before

A central bank conducts monetary policy by influencing interest rates, credit conditions, and the money supply. Lower rates can encourage borrowing and spending during a slowdown, while tighter policy can restrain inflation at the cost of weaker demand and employment.

After

A big bank helps manage money by changing interest rates and the amount of money. Lower rates help people borrow and spend when the economy is slow, while tighter rules can stop inflation but might mean less spending and jobs.

Example 89

Before

Fiscal policy changes government spending and taxation to influence economic activity. Deficit spending can support demand during recessions, but its impact depends on timing, resource availability, household and business responses, financing costs, and how the spending or tax changes are designed.

After

Fiscal policy is when the government changes its spending and taxes to help the economy. Deficit spending can help during tough times, but it depends on when they make the changes, how people and businesses react, and how they make the changes.

Example 90

Before

Banks connect savers with borrowers while providing payment services and liquidity. Because they fund longer-term loans partly with deposits withdrawable on demand, confidence and regulation matter; capital requirements, deposit insurance, and central-bank lending aim to reduce the risk of destabilizing runs.

After

Banks help people who save money find people who need loans. They also make it easy to use money quickly. Because banks use money people save to pay for bigger loans, they need to be safe and regulated so people don't worry about losing their savings.

Example 91

Before

A monopoly is a single seller protected by barriers that limit entry. It can restrict output and charge above competitive cost, creating deadweight loss, although regulation must consider innovation, economies of scale, product quality, and whether potential competitors can realistically enter.

After

A monopoly is when one person is the only one selling something. They can control how much they sell and how much they charge. But we need to think about things like innovation, good quality, and if other people can join them.

Example 92

Before

An externality occurs when an action imposes costs or benefits on people outside the transaction. Pollution is a negative externality and vaccination can create positive spillovers, so taxes, subsidies, regulation, or tradable permits may help align private incentives with social effects.

After

An externality is when someone's actions affect other people who didn't agree to it. For example, pollution can make the air bad, and vaccinations can make people healthier. We can use money or special rules to make things better for everyone.

Example 93

Before

Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, meaning people cannot easily be prevented from using them and one person's use does not substantially reduce another's. These features encourage free riding, so markets may provide less than the socially desired amount without collective funding.

After

Public goods are things that everyone can use, like a park or a playground. They don't make it harder for someone else to use them. But if people don't all work together and pay for them together, there might not be enough for everyone.

Example 94

Before

Asymmetric information arises when one party to a transaction knows more than another. Adverse selection affects who enters a market before an agreement, while moral hazard changes behavior afterward; screening, signaling, warranties, deductibles, and reputation can reduce these problems.

After

Asymmetric information happens when one person knows more than another. Adverse selection impacts who joins a group before a deal, and moral hazard changes behavior afterward. Screening, signaling, warranties, deductibles, and reputation can help fix these problems.

Example 95

Before

Human capital consists of knowledge, skills, health, and experience that raise a person's productive capacity. Education and training can increase future earnings and economic output, but their returns vary with quality, labor demand, access, and the opportunity cost of time spent learning.

After

Human capital means what people know and do that helps them be successful. If you learn new things, you can make more money and help the economy grow. But it depends on how well you learn, what people need, and how much you spend.

Example 96

Before

Economic growth can come from a larger workforce, more physical capital, improved human capital, and technological progress. Sustained increases in living standards depend especially on productivity, because simply adding workers or machines eventually faces diminishing returns without better methods and ideas.

After

Economic growth can happen when more people are working, there are more things, people are smarter, and new things are made. To keep the economy growing, we need to be more productive and find better ways to do things more efficiently.

Example 97

Before

Exchange rates state the price of one currency in terms of another. Demand for currencies responds to trade, investment, interest rates, expectations, and policy, while appreciation makes imports cheaper for domestic buyers but can make exports more expensive abroad.

After

Exchange rates are like the price of one coin compared to another. People want different currencies because of things like trading and saving. When a currency gets cheaper, it makes buying things cheaper but can make selling things more expensive.

Example 98

Before

Income inequality describes how unevenly earnings and other income are distributed, while wealth inequality concerns accumulated assets and debts. Taxes, transfers, education, labor institutions, technology, inheritance, and asset prices all influence these distributions and the mobility between them.

After

Income inequality means that some people make more money than others. Wealth inequality is about how much money and other things people have. Things like taxes, schools, jobs, technology, and gifts can affect how much money different people have.

Example 99

Before

Game theory studies strategic choices in which each participant's outcome depends on what others do. A Nash equilibrium occurs when no player can gain by changing strategy alone, though the equilibrium may still be worse for everyone than a coordinated alternative.

After

Game theory is about how people make choices based on what other people do. A Nash equilibrium is when no one can do better by changing their choice, even if the outcome might be worse than if everyone worked together.

Example 100

Before

Behavioral economics examines systematic departures from perfectly rational decision-making. Loss aversion, present bias, limited attention, framing, and social norms can influence choices, so small changes in defaults or information presentation may alter behavior without removing options.

After

Behavioral economics studies how people make decisions that aren't always the best. It looks at loss aversion, present bias, and social norms. Sometimes, small changes in the way information is presented can change our choices.
